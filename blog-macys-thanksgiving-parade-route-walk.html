Walking the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade route before the crowds arrive

✎
Written by the Urban Tales team
November 2, 2026 · 6 min read
→ This walk is in the app as a free 4-stop route along the parade's exact course, from the Museum of Natural History to Macy's. See it ↓

In 1927, the parade's star attraction wasn't a balloon. It was live bears, elephants and camels borrowed from the Central Park Zoo — and Macy's dropped them within three years because they scared the children watching from the curb.

That trade, zoo animals for balloons, is the first turn on a walk most New Yorkers already half-know: the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade route, about 2.5 miles down the spine of Manhattan from a museum on the Upper West Side to a department store at Herald Square. 2026 is the 100th parade, even though the first one ran in 1924 — the gap is three wartime years, 1942 to 1944, when Macy's gave its balloon rubber and the country's helium to the war effort instead. You don't need a parade permit, a float, or a 6am alarm to walk the route itself. You just need a free morning and the stops below.

A museum block turns into a balloon garage

The walk starts at West 77th Street and Central Park West, outside the American Museum of Natural History — the parade's official start line, and for one afternoon a year, a balloon garage. The day before Thanksgiving, handlers lay the giant character balloons flat across the closed block and inflate them under cargo nets, weighted down against the wind. Watching is free from the sidewalk; marching inside the netting the next morning is not. That takes a Macy's employee willing to sponsor you, a mandatory safety class, and, by long-standing rule, a minimum body weight of around 120 pounds, so a gust can't lift the handler along with the balloon.

Tip

If you're here the afternoon before Thanksgiving, the public viewing stretch is the sidewalk on 77th and 81st Streets between Central Park West and Columbus Avenue, roughly noon to 6pm. Columbus Circle bends around a column older than the parade

From the museum, the route runs south down Central Park West to Columbus Circle, circling the 76-foot granite column at its center before turning east onto Central Park South. An Italian-language newspaper, Il Progresso, paid for the column in 1892 to mark 400 years since Columbus's voyage; when the subway was dug under the circle nine years later, the column spent a stretch propped up on wooden stilts rather than come down. It's cleared every parade since without most marchers noticing it's there.

Nineteen blocks of Sixth Avenue, with one real landmark

East on Central Park South, then south down Sixth Avenue for the parade's longest unbroken stretch, past Radio City Music Hall around West 50th Street. Radio City opened in 1932, the same year Macy's finally stopped releasing its balloons into the open sky at the parade's end — a tradition that had offered a cash reward to whoever mailed the scraps back, until one balloon wrapped around a student pilot's wing over Long Island and sent her into a tailspin. Walk the route on an ordinary day and there's nothing to see at Radio City but the marquee; the parade itself never goes inside, it just passes the door.

Herald Square, where the walk — and the parade — has always ended

The route turns west on 34th Street for its final two blocks, ending at Macy's flagship store, which still fills nearly the entire block between Broadway, Seventh Avenue, 34th and 35th Streets. The store had already been the largest in the United States for over two decades by the time the first parade used its doors as a finish line in 1924. On parade morning itself, this is also where the crowd gets several rows deep fastest; the stretch back near the Upper West Side start empties out first once the floats have passed.

Walk it on an ordinary Tuesday and it's four landmarks with maybe twenty minutes of actual walking between them, spread over roughly 2.5 miles — the same ground covered on Urban Tales' New York walks any day of the year. Walk it on the fourth Thursday of November and it's the same four landmarks, just with a hundred years of parade standing between you and the sidewalk.
