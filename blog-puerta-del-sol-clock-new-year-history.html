The clock Madrid watches was never built to be one

✎
Written by the Urban Tales team
November 20, 2026 · 5 min read
3 Puerta del Sol's clock is stop 3 of a free 5-stop walk from the Cibeles Fountain down to the Royal Palace. See the route ↓

Every December 31, something like 15 million people across Spain hold twelve grapes in one hand and watch the Puerta del Sol clock tell them when to eat each one. That clock sits on top of a building that was built to sort the mail, not keep time.

The building is the Real Casa de Correos, Madrid's old royal post and telegraph office, finished in 1768 under King Charles III to architect Jaime Marquet's design. It handled letters and dispatches for more than a century before anyone thought to put a clock on it. Today it's the seat of the regional government of Madrid, and almost nobody who watches it on New Year's Eve knows that history — they're too busy counting chimes.

A gate that isn't there anymore

The square itself is named for something that no longer exists. Puerta del Sol — "Gate of the Sun" — takes its name from a medieval city gate that once stood roughly where the post office does now, facing east, catching the morning light on its painted sun. The gate came down in the 16th century as Madrid outgrew its old walls. The name stuck to the empty space long after the stone did, which is part of why first-time visitors often arrive expecting an actual gate and find a clock instead.

A watchmaker's gift, not a government decision

The Puerta del Sol clock people actually watch wasn't commissioned by the Crown or the city council. It was a gift. José Rodríguez Losada, a Spanish watchmaker who'd built a fortune running a clock shop on the Strand in London, donated a new precision mechanism to Madrid in 1866 to replace the tower's older clock, which had a reputation for running fast, slow, or not at all. Losada's mechanism — rebuilt and restored since, but still the one keeping time in that tower — is the reason the whole country can trust a single clock enough to build a national ritual around it.

"The most-watched clock in Spain wasn't a state project. It was one émigré's apology for the one it replaced."

The chimes everyone gets wrong on their first try

The mechanism doesn't go straight to midnight. First come four short preliminary chimes, the "cuartos," spaced a few seconds apart — then, after a brief pause, the twelve "campanadas" that actually mark the hour. First-timers routinely start eating on the cuartos and finish their grapes long before midnight, or lose count and panic halfway through. Spanish TV broadcasts have spent decades trying to make the distinction obvious on screen, because the square itself, packed with tens of thousands of people, gives you no second chance to start over.

If you ever try this in the square yourself: let the four short cuartos finish completely before you touch a grape. The real twelve start right after, about one per second — with Urban Tales running, the clock's own story plays as you reach Puerta del Sol, well before the countdown ever starts.

Why grapes, and why twelve

Nobody can point to one official origin for the twelve grapes. The version most often repeated traces it to around 1909, when vineyards around Alicante and Murcia had an unusually large harvest and needed to move stock fast; selling bunches marketed as "lucky grapes," one for each coming month, eaten at the stroke of midnight, did the job. A second, older thread holds that Madrid's upper classes had already been mocking the city's aspiring bourgeoisie for imitating a fashionable French New Year's habit of grapes and champagne — and that the mockery itself, performed loudly in public, is what actually taught the habit to everyone watching. Historians lean toward the grape surplus as the practical cause. Madrileños tell both.

Whichever came first, the habit outgrew the square decades ago. Spanish state television has broadcast the midnight chimes live from Puerta del Sol since the 1960s, and the hosts who deliver the countdown each year — usually a familiar news anchor paired with a guest — become minor celebrities for exactly one night. Streets around the square close to traffic hours in advance; by midnight the crowd physically present numbers in the tens of thousands, with tens of millions more watching the same clock face from their own kitchens.

So on December 31, the building the whole country has its eyes on isn't a palace, a cathedral, or anything built to be looked at. It's a repurposed mail office with a donated clock, standing in a square named after a gate that was torn down four centuries ago — which is, roughly, the whole point of a hidden story: the thing everyone already recognizes almost never turns out to be what they think it is.
