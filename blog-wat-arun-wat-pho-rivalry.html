The grudge behind Wat Arun and Wat Pho

✎
Written by the Urban Tales team
October 12, 2026 · 6 min read

The white spire everyone photographs at Wat Arun is covered in broken plates. Literally — shattered Chinese dinnerware that ships once dumped as ballast, pressed into the walls of a temple built by the dynasty that had just overthrown the king who gave it its name.

Stand on the Thonburi side of the Chao Phraya River at Wat Arun and look across the water. You're looking at Wat Pho, and at the Grand Palace beside it. The two temples face each other across a few hundred meters of brown river water, and the real Wat Arun Wat Pho history is less about architecture than about who got to keep the throne.

A temple named by a king who didn't get to keep it

Wat Arun existed long before it had that name. Under the old Ayutthaya kingdom it was a modest riverside temple called Wat Makok, after the olive trees nearby. When Ayutthaya fell to Burmese forces in 1767, a general named Taksin regrouped what was left of the Siamese army, declared himself king, and built a new capital at Thonburi — right next to this temple. Legend says he passed it at dawn on his way upriver and was struck by the light on the water; he renamed it Wat Chaeng, "Temple of Dawn," and made it part of his own palace grounds.

Taksin didn't just rebuild a kingdom here. He installed the Emerald Buddha — Thailand's most sacred image, captured from Vientiane — inside this temple, making it, for a few years, the spiritual center of the entire country.

"The temple that once held Thailand's holiest statue spent the next two centuries on the wrong side of the river from power."

Fifteen years, then a palace coup

Taksin's reign ended badly. By most accounts he grew erratic, reportedly believing himself a living Buddhist saint and demanding monks pay him homage — a claim serious enough that senior clergy pushed back. In 1782 he was deposed and executed, and his own general, Chao Phraya Chakri, took the throne as Rama I, founder of the dynasty that still reigns in Thailand today.

Rama I's first move was geographic as much as political: he crossed the river and built his new capital, and a new Grand Palace, on the Bangkok side — leaving Taksin's old palace and temple behind in Thonburi. The Emerald Buddha went with him, installed in a new temple, Wat Phra Kaew, inside the new palace walls. Wat Arun kept its name and its riverside view. It lost everything else that had made it the center of the country.

The new dynasty built its answer right next to the palace

Wat Pho predates both kings — a temple had stood on that site since the 1600s — but Rama I rebuilt it from the ground up as his royal temple the moment his new palace went up beside it, and his successors kept adding to it for decades. The showpiece came under Rama III in 1832: a reclining Buddha 46 meters long and 15 meters high, so large it barely fits inside its own hall. Its feet alone are inlaid with mother-of-pearl in 108 auspicious patterns — a level of detail you only commission for something meant to outlast you.

Meanwhile Wat Arun sat across the water, no longer the royal temple, until Rama II and then Rama III got around to rebuilding its central spire too — raising it to roughly 70 meters, taller than almost anything else in the old capital. It's hard not to read that as a second generation finishing a fight the first generation started by simply moving away.

Even the glitter was secondhand

Here's the detail that changes how the spire looks once you know it: the thousands of porcelain shards covering Wat Arun aren't fine art. Most of it is broken Chinese crockery — plates, bowls, cups — that trading ships used as ballast on the voyage from China and discarded once they reached Bangkok's port. Workers pressed the shards into the stucco by hand, piece by piece, flower by flower. The most photographed monument in Thonburi is largely built from someone else's garbage, which somehow makes it more impressive, not less.

The two temples still face each other across the same stretch of river they always have. Most people cross between them on a ferry that costs a few baht, snap the same two photos, and never hear why one has a palace's worth of gold leaf and the other is wearing someone else's broken dishes. The reason is a coup that happened almost 250 years ago, and neither temple has ever bothered to mention it.
