What's still standing at the Brandenburg Gate — and what isn't

✎
Written by the Urban Tales team
September 23, 2026 · 6 min read

The horses on top of the Brandenburg Gate have been stolen by an emperor, melted down by Allied bombs, and quietly stripped of a cross by a government that didn't want to look too Prussian. None of that shows up on the average five-minute photo stop.

Ask ten people standing under the gate what they know about it and most will mention the Berlin Wall, which isn't wrong — it ran a few hundred meters north of here for 28 years — but it undersells the gate's own Brandenburg Gate history, which starts in 1791, gets robbed by Napoleon in 1806, and doesn't really finish until three years after German reunification, when two governments finally agreed on what the statue on top was allowed to be holding.

The museum director who stole a chariot

In late 1806, after Napoleon crushed Prussia at the Battle of Jena-Auerstedt, French troops occupied Berlin. Vivant Denon — director of Napoleon's new museum in Paris, the building that would eventually become the Louvre — had the bronze Quadriga taken down piece by piece and crated for the trip to Paris. She sat there for eight years, still boxed up, never actually put on display as the trophy she was meant to be. Somebody in Paris apparently never got around to unpacking her.

Blücher gets her back

When Prussian troops entered Paris in 1814, General Gebhard Leberecht Blücher's men found the crates untouched in a Louvre storeroom. King Frederick William III rode his cavalry back through the gate on August 7, 1814, and the statue went up again — except she wasn't quite the same statue anymore. She'd originally carried an olive wreath, as the goddess of Peace. Berlin sent her back up holding a Roman military standard instead, with a Prussian eagle and an Iron Cross welded to it. The monument that left in 1806 as a peace symbol came home dressed for war.

"The statue that went to Paris was a goddess of Peace. The one that came back was dressed for war."

What survived 1945

By the end of World War II, the gate and the Quadriga were almost gone. Allied bombing and the street fighting for Berlin melted the goddess's figure entirely and tore the chariot and horses into pieces too small to rebuild from. The only original fragment left is the head of one horse, dug out of rubble near the old Berlin Zoo — it's behind glass at the Märkisches Museum a few kilometers away now, the single piece of the statue that's actually from 1791.

What saved the design wasn't luck. In 1942, someone in the city administration had plaster casts made of the horses and the goddess, years before the bombing that would make those molds irreplaceable. When Berlin finally recast the Quadriga in copper in 1957 and 1958, both halves of the divided city worked from those same molds — East Berlin repaired the gate's stonework, West Berlin funded and cast the statue, and for a couple of years two governments that otherwise refused to speak to each other were quietly finishing the same sculpture.

An emblem East Berlin didn't want overhead

In August 1958, East German authorities had the finished statue moved overnight to a workshop at the Neue Marstall and had the Iron Cross and Prussian eagle stripped off the goddess's staff before it went back on the gate. Prussian militarism, in the East's telling, had no business over a socialist skyline. For the next 33 years, the Quadriga stood over the gate holding a bare staff.

Then in 1961 came the Wall, close enough that the gate ended up standing inside the death strip itself — technically in East Berlin, floodlit, patrolled, and unreachable from either side. West Berliners could climb a wooden viewing platform a block away and look at it through the barrier; East Berliners weren't allowed anywhere near it either. It stayed that way for 28 years, a monument nobody could actually stand under, until just after midnight on December 22, 1989, when workers finally opened a gap in the barriers and the first crowds walked through.

The Iron Cross didn't come back that night. It took until 1991, after reunification and one more restoration, for the goddess to get her cross and eagle back — the first time since 1958 that the statue on the gate matched the one that rode home from Paris in 1814.

Stand under the gate now, on the side that used to be the death strip, and none of that shows from the ground — she just looks like bronze on stone, the way she has on every postcard since 1991. But you're looking at a copy of a copy, cast from thirty-year-old plaster molds by two governments that refused to speak to each other, missing her cross for longer than most of the DDR existed.
