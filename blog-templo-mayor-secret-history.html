What's still buried under Mexico City's Zócalo

✎
Written by the Urban Tales team
September 2, 2026 · 6 min read

On February 21, 1978, two electric company workers digging a routine cable trench a block from the Zócalo hit something that wasn't supposed to be there: a carved stone disk, roughly 11 feet across, showing a woman torn into pieces.

They'd found the Coyolxauhqui stone, and with it, the buried heart of the Aztec empire's most important temple. That discovery is the reason the Templo Mayor history most guides tell you starts in 1978 — not because the temple is new, but because for 450 years, nobody in the city walking to work above it knew it was still down there.

The workers weren't looking for a temple

The crew was laying underground cable for the electric utility near the corner of Guatemala and Argentina streets, a few minutes' walk from the cathedral. Roughly eight feet down, their shovels hit stone that was clearly carved, not just quarried. Archaeologists were called in the same week. What they eventually uncovered wasn't a wall or a foundation — it was one of the seven layers of the Templo Mayor, the twin-shrined pyramid that once stood at the exact center of Tenochtitlan, the Aztec capital that Mexico City was built directly on top of.

"The Spanish didn't just conquer the temple. They buried it under a city and built a cathedral where anyone standing on it could no longer tell it had existed."

Why nobody noticed for 450 years

After Tenochtitlan fell in 1521, the temple wasn't simply left to crumble — it was dismantled on purpose, its stones reused to build the colonial city springing up around it, including parts of the cathedral itself. Streets, government buildings, and eventually a modern city were laid directly over the rubble. By the time archaeologist Eduardo Matos Moctezuma led the dig that followed the Coyolxauhqui find, thirteen colonial-era buildings had to come down just to expose one city block of what was underneath.

The temple itself turned out to be nested like a set of Russian dolls — Aztec rulers had rebuilt and enlarged it roughly every fifty years, burying the previous version inside the new one rather than tearing it down. The layer the electricians happened to hit was one of the later, outer shells.

What's still down there

Only a fraction of the original sacred precinct has ever been excavated, because the rest sits directly under a working, UNESCO-listed historic center — you can't demolish the cathedral to keep digging. Most of what remains of the Aztecs' central temple complex is permanently sealed under streets, plazas, and buildings people walk across every day without a second thought. The site that was uncovered is now the Museo del Templo Mayor, which opened in 1987, and the Coyolxauhqui stone that started it all sits at the base of the excavated temple, exactly where the dig found her.

So the next time you're standing in the Zócalo watching flag ceremonies or waiting for a table, it's worth remembering that the ground under your feet isn't just old. It's stacked — colonial city on top of Aztec temple on top of Aztec temple, most of it still down there, unopened.
