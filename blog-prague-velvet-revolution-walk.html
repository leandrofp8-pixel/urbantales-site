A self-guided walk through Prague's Velvet Revolution

✎
Written by the Urban Tales team
October 19, 2026 · 5 min read

On the evening of 17 November 1989, riot police cornered a few thousand students on a 500-metre street in Prague and beat them with batons until there was nowhere left to run. Eight days later, hundreds of thousands of people filled the same part of the city to hear a playwright who'd been in prison a year earlier speak from a balcony above a shop front. This self-guided Velvet Revolution walk covers both places, and the two more in between, in about twenty minutes on foot.

Start outside the National Theatre, at the river end of Národní třída. It's not part of the 1989 story directly, but it anchors the street: Prague rebuilt it by public subscription in under two years after it burned down just before its 1881 opening, which is the kind of civic stubbornness that shows up again later on this walk. From here, head up Národní třída toward Wenceslas Square.

The street where it started

On 17 November, students marching to mark the 50th anniversary of the Nazi closure of Czech universities turned onto this street and found it sealed at both ends. Riot police moved in from the front and the rear at once, so there was no way out — accounts describe students raising empty hands and showing the "V" sign as the batons came down. No one died that night, despite a false rumor that spread within hours that a student had been killed; the rumor itself, more than the beating, is often credited with turning a student protest into a nationwide one.

About halfway up, at Národní 16, look down at the pavement rather than up at the shop signs. A small bronze plaque shows eight hands reaching up in different gestures — a fist, an open palm, the "V" — dated simply "17.11.89." It's easy to walk past; there's no large monument, just this one marker set into the stone outside what's now a bar association building. Every November 17th, which is a public holiday in the Czech Republic, Praguers still leave candles and flowers here.

The balcony above the shop front

Keep walking and Národní třída opens straight into the bottom of Wenceslas Square. Look up and to the left, at Wenceslas Square 36: a stone balcony juts out above what was then the Melantrich publishing house. Around a week after the beating, Václav Havel stepped onto it alongside Alexander Dubček — the reformist leader the Soviets had removed after crushing the 1968 Prague Spring, brought back out twenty-one years later to stand next to the man who'd lead the country after him. Below them, the crowd jingled house keys in the air, a gesture that meant, roughly, your time is up.

The building now holds a clothing store, and the balcony is easy to miss if you don't already know to look for it — there's no plaque at street level marking what happened above the awning.

Under the horse's tail

Walk up the square's gentle slope toward the equestrian statue of St. Wenceslas at its top end. Praguers have a nickname for this spot — "pod ocasem," under the tail — and for ten days in November 1989 it was the city's unofficial town square. The opposition group Civic Forum read its daily demands to the crowd from here, and it's where word spread on 27 November that the two-hour general strike had worked: factories across the country had stopped, and the government's resignation was now a matter of days, not weeks.

What the museum still won't let you forget

At the top of the square, the National Museum's facade still carries small pockmarks from August 1968 — Soviet tanks fired on the building during the invasion that ended the Prague Spring, reportedly mistaking it for the national radio headquarters nearby. The scars were left unrepaired for decades as a quiet, unofficial memorial; a 2018 renovation preserved a section of them behind glass, to the left of the main entrance.

Set into the pavement in front of the steps, slightly off-center, is a small bronze cross that most visitors walk straight over. It marks where Jan Palach, a 20-year-old philosophy student, set himself on fire in January 1969 to protest the occupation and what he saw as the public's resignation to it. Jan Zajíc did the same a month later; both names are on the cross. Their deaths happened twenty years before the Velvet Revolution, a few hundred metres from where the crowds would eventually gather to finish what they'd started.
