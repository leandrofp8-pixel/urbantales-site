Hidden Story · Barcelona

Sagrada Família just topped out. For 136 years, it had no permit to build it.

✎
Written by the Urban Tales team
November 16, 2026 · 6 min read

On February 20, 2026, a crane lowered the final piece of a cross onto Sagrada Família's central tower, bringing the building to its full height of 172.5 metres and making it the tallest church on Earth. Barcelona had been waiting 144 years for that moment. For 136 of them, the basilica didn't have a building permit.

That isn't a joke about Spanish bureaucracy or a slow-moving construction site. It's a real, documented fact: for most of its existence, the most famous unfinished building in the world was, on paper, unauthorized. Nobody noticed for over a century, because nobody was in a position to ask.

A town that wasn't Barcelona yet

Construction began in 1882 on a plot that, at the time, sat just outside Barcelona's city limits, in a separate, independent municipality called Sant Martí de Provençals. A local architect named Francisco de Paula del Villar started the project; Antoni Gaudí, then 31, took it over the following year after a dispute over the design of the crypt. Sant Martí was swallowed into Barcelona proper in 1897, fifteen years into construction — and whatever paperwork existed before that merger never got reconciled with the city it now belonged to.

The Sagrada Família Foundation's position, for decades, was that a permit had in fact been granted back in 1885 by Sant Martí's own town hall, before the annexation. Barcelona's city council didn't recognize it as valid for a basilica of this size, in this location, under its own rules. The two sides simply left the question alone — through two world wars, a civil war, and a century of scaffolding.

"For well over a century, the tallest building under construction in Barcelona was, technically, missing the city's permission to exist."

Part of why nobody forced the issue is money, or rather the lack of a government hand in it. Sagrada Família has never taken a cent of public funding — not from the city, the region, or the Spanish state. It's been paid for entirely by private donations and, for the last several decades, ticket sales from the roughly five million visitors who climb through it every year. With no public budget attached, there was no routine audit to trip over the missing permit.

The bill came due in 2018

That changed once Barcelona's city council took a harder look at the site's paperwork and confirmed what outside reporting had already started asking about: there was no valid modern building permit on file. In October 2018, the Sagrada Família Foundation agreed to pay the city €36 million over ten years — money earmarked for metro access, street repairs and security around a site that draws millions of people a year but had never formally funded any of it. A further €4.6 million covered the permit application itself.

The permit was finally issued in June 2019. It's valid for seven years, which is how 2026 — the centenary of Gaudí's death — became the semi-official target for finishing the building's structural form, and why this year's completed tower lands on a date that was picked on purpose.

Look at the stonework on the Nativity façade, the oldest completed section, finished to Gaudí's own design in the 1930s: it's darker, denser and more weathered than the newer towers rising beside it. Why the tower stops exactly where it does

Gaudí died in 1926, hit by a tram on his way to confession, having seen only the crypt and one façade completed. He left behind plaster models and sketches for the rest, including a deliberate height limit for the central tower: 172.5 metres, a touch short of Montjuïc, the hill that forms Barcelona's highest natural point. The story goes that Gaudí refused to let anything he built rise above what nature had already put there. Whether that's the literal reason or a tidy legend attached to it afterward, the number has held for a century of engineers who never got to ask him directly.

The structure is done, but the building isn't. Interior work, smaller chapels and decorative stonework are expected to run through 2028. If you're standing underneath it this year, you're looking at the exact moment a 144-year argument between art, money and a missing piece of paper finally ran out of road.
