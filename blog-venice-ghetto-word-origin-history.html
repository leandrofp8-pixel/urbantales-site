Where the word "ghetto" actually came from

✎
Written by the Urban Tales team
October 21, 2026 · 6 min read
2 Venice's Ghetto is stop 2 of a free 6-stop walk through Cannaregio to the Rialto Bridge. See the route ↓

In 1516, Venice decided where its Jewish residents could live, walled it in, and locked the gates every night. The word for that island — ghèto — wasn't an insult. It was Venetian dialect for a foundry, and the Republic had been casting cannons there for a century before a single Jewish family moved in.

The site sat in a quiet corner of Cannaregio, across a narrow canal from the rest of Venice, reachable only by two bridges the city could seal shut. A copper foundry had worked that spot since at least the 1300s, melting metal for the Republic's naval guns — in the local dialect, the pouring and casting was called getto, from gettare, to cast or to throw. By 1516 the furnace was long gone, but the name had stuck to the ground itself. When Venice needed somewhere to confine its Jewish community that year, this already-named island was the obvious, cheapest choice. Nobody coined a word for exclusion. The city just kept calling the place what it had always been called, and the name travelled with everyone who was forced to live there.

A neighborhood that had nowhere to go but up

The 1516 decree moved roughly 700 people onto an island built for a handful of foundry workers. Within a few decades that population had more than doubled, and the island couldn't grow outward — the canal and the walls were fixed. So Venice's Ghetto did something almost no other European neighborhood was doing yet: it built upward. Apartment blocks rose seven and eight storeys over tiny footprints, with ceilings low enough that residents had to duck, among the tallest buildings in Venice at the time. It's one of the earliest dense, vertical housing stocks in Europe — built not out of ambition, but because up was the only direction left.

The guards the residents paid for themselves

Two bridges connected the island to the rest of the city, and both were gated at sunset and reopened at dawn, watched overnight by Christian guards patrolling the surrounding canal by boat. The detail most tours skip: the Jewish community itself was required to cover those guards' wages. Being locked in was never free.

The wall that shut the Ghetto's gates at night was the same wall that, over nearly three centuries of occasional mob violence elsewhere in Venice, residents came to rely on as a kind of protection.

Five synagogues you can't spot from the street

Venetian law didn't allow synagogues to look like synagogues. No dome, no facade announcing a house of worship — each scola was built into the upper floor of an ordinary-looking apartment block, indistinguishable from the building next door. The oldest, the Scola Grande Tedesca, was finished in 1528 for the Ghetto's German-speaking community. Four more followed over the next century: an Italian congregation, and two serving Sephardic Jews who had fled Spain and Portugal, the Scola Spagnola and the Scola Levantina. Those last two are the only ones still holding regular services today.

The word outlived the wall by centuries

The physical gates came down in 1797, when Napoleon's troops took Venice and dismantled the old Republic's barriers along with most of its institutions. For the first time in nearly 300 years, Jewish residents were legally free to live anywhere in the city. Most of the five synagogues stayed exactly where they'd always been, still tucked inside buildings that look like nothing from the street.

Venice's Jewish Museum, which runs guided tours through the synagogues, has been closed since 2020 for a nine-million-euro restoration — expanding the complex and bringing three of the Ghetto's most important Renaissance synagogues, the Scola Tedesca, Scola Canton, and the long-inaccessible Scola Italiana, back into public view after six years. It's scheduled to reopen on October 22, 2026, with the Italian synagogue, tucked inside a private residential building, rejoining the museum's circuit for the first time in years.

The word that came out of this island outlived the wall. By the 19th century, ghetto had drifted out of Venetian dialect and into English, German, and a dozen other languages to describe any segregated urban quarter — stripped, somewhere along the way, of the fact that its first meaning had nothing to do with religion or race at all. It meant a foundry. Venice just happened to need somewhere to put a wall.
