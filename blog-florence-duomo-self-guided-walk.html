A self-guided Duomo walk that skips Florence's worst lines

✎
Written by the Urban Tales team
September 14, 2026 · 5 min read

Filippo Brunelleschi built the largest masonry dome ever raised without a single scaffold holding it up from the ground, and he refused to explain how he did it to anyone while he was alive. The line to climb inside it now regularly takes longer than his whole secret is worth explaining.

This is a self-guided Duomo walk built around one idea: almost everything worth seeing in this stretch of Florence sits within five minutes of the cathedral, and nearly all of it is quiet before 9am. We got to Piazza del Duomo a little after 7, expecting to share it with someone. We didn't — just a street cleaner working the steps and a nun photographing the facade on her phone.

The dome that sat open for fifty years

Florence's cathedral had a hole in its roof for half a century because nobody alive knew how to close a span that wide without scaffolding rising from the floor of the nave. Brunelleschi won the job in 1418, invented the hoists and the herringbone brick pattern to do it, and died before it was finished — still telling no one his method. Standing under it at 7am, before the guided groups start pointing up in unison, it's oddly easy to believe an entire city just had to trust one stubborn man for sixteen years.

Twenty-seven years on one set of doors

Around the corner, the Baptistery's bronze east doors are the reason "gorgeous" gets overused in every Florence guidebook ever written. Lorenzo Ghiberti won the commission in 1425 and didn't finish the ten panels until 1452 — Michelangelo is the one credited with nicknaming them the Gates of Paradise, and the name stuck hard enough that nobody in the square this morning called them anything else.

Booking ahead beats waiting it out

The dome climb is timed-entry only — you book a slot in advance rather than queue same-day — and by 9am the walk-up line for people without a reservation was already curling past the Baptistery. We'd booked a 7:30 slot two days earlier online and were most of the way up 463 steps of exposed brick before that line had even started forming downstairs.

A wronged husband's revenge, carved in stone

On the cathedral's north side, at the Porta della Mandorla, there's a small ox head carved into the stonework that the official story says honors the animals that hauled the marble. The local version is pettier: a foreman caught having an affair with a baker's wife allegedly had it carved facing the baker's own shopfront, horns first, so he'd see it every morning. Historians wave it off. We stood there anyway, trying to work out which building across the street the shop would have been.

The square where a monk was burned on his own spot

Piazza della Signoria used to be the whole of Florence's government in one square. In 1497, the friar Girolamo Savonarola convinced the city to pile up its books, mirrors, and paintings and burn them here. The following year, Florence turned on him and burned him in the same place. A small round marble disk, barely a foot across and completely unlabeled, marks the exact spot — and on a normal afternoon it sits under a few hundred people who never once look down.

The bridge Hitler didn't blow up

Every other bridge over the Arno was destroyed by retreating German forces in August 1944. Ponte Vecchio is the single exception, and the leading theory credits a direct order from Hitler, who'd visited Florence in 1938 and reportedly found it too beautiful to lose. It used to be lined with butchers until 1593, when the Medici evicted them all for the smell and moved in goldsmiths instead — which is why every shop on it still sells jewelry, to a bridge that at 8am had almost nobody else on it.

By 9:30, the streets around the Duomo had turned into the Florence you've seen in every photo — shoulder to shoulder, a line for the doors alone. We were already across the river with coffee, having covered the whole route with room to actually stop and look.
