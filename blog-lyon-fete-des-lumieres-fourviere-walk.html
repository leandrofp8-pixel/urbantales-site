City Guide · Lyon

A self-guided Lyon walk, from Vieux Lyon's traboules up to Fourvière

✎
Written by the Urban Tales team
November 18, 2026 · 6 min read

Every December 8, Lyon fills tens of thousands of windows with small glass jars and lit candles. The tradition traces back to 1852, when a flood pushed back a statue's unveiling by three months — and the rescheduled ceremony nearly got rained out too.

This year's Fête des Lumières runs December 5 to 8, 2026, built around that same date and the same hill. The free event draws well over a million people into Lyon's streets for four nights of light installations projected onto building façades across the city. But the self-guided Lyon walk that actually explains why any of it happens starts well before dark, in the old quarter below Fourvière hill, and ends at the spot where the whole tradition began.

A vow against the plague, then one against a flood

The story starts in 1643, when Lyon's council vowed to build the Virgin Mary a statue on Fourvière hill if she spared the city from the plague circling the region at the time. The vow sat mostly unfulfilled for two centuries, until the city finally commissioned the statue in 1852 — only for a flood on the Saône to delay its delivery three months past the planned unveiling. When the rescheduled ceremony on December 8 was threatened by a storm that evening, Lyonnais lit candles in their windows anyway, grateful the rain had held off long enough for the fireworks. The custom never really stopped.

"Lyon's biggest light show didn't start with lasers or sponsors. It started with ordinary people deciding a few candles were enough."

Where to start, and what's hiding behind the doors

Place Bellecour is the easiest place to get your bearings: one of Europe's largest open squares, with a 19th-century bronze of Louis XIV on horseback at its center and the whole city radiating outward from it. From here, head north into Vieux Lyon, the Renaissance quarter squeezed between the Saône and the hill. Its real secret isn't a building — it's what runs through the buildings. Lyon has roughly 40 traboules open to walkers, covered passageways that cut straight through courtyards and stairwells, built by silk merchants centuries ago to move fabric between streets without getting it wet in the rain. The Longue Traboule, running from 27 Rue Saint-Jean through to 54 Rue du Bœuf, crosses four separate courtyards and was used by the French Resistance to move through the district unseen during the German occupation. Most of the doors you'll pass look like someone's private entrance. Some still are.

Tip

Traboules aren't marked on the street — look for a small round plaque near the doorway reading "traboule," and push. With Urban Tales running, the Longue Traboule's Resistance history plays as you reach it, so you're not guessing which unmarked door it's behind.

Up the hill, the easy way or the honest way

From Vieux Lyon, the quickest way up to Fourvière is the funicular locals call "la ficelle" — the string — which has carried passengers up this hill since 1900 and takes about two minutes from the Vieux-Lyon station. If you'd rather walk, the staircase behind the Saint-Paul church runs several hundred steps, depending on which flight you take, and it is genuinely steep; the gentler option is the switchback path through the Jardins du Rosaire, which takes closer to 25 minutes on foot. Neither way is wrong. One just costs a ticket, and the other costs your calves.

The basilica that isn't the old vow

The basilica waiting at the top isn't the one from the 1852 story — that one was a single statue, which still stands beside it. The Basilique Notre-Dame de Fourvière came later, built between 1872 and 1896 after Lyon's leaders made a separate vow: spare the city from the Prussian army then advancing on France, and they'd build something far larger. The Prussians turned back before reaching Lyon, the basilica was paid for entirely with private donations, and the result is the layered, almost fortress-like church that now shares the hilltop with the statue it was built beside.

The view, and the ruins underneath it

Step onto the esplanade in front of the basilica and the reason it sits exactly here becomes obvious: the view runs out over every roof in Lyon, with the Saône curving below toward its meeting point with the Rhône. During the Fête des Lumières, this is where a good share of the crowd ends up facing the city for the final night's display. A short walk around the side of the hill leads to the Théâtre antique de Fourvière, a Roman theatre cut into the hillside in the 1st century BC that still seats an audience every summer for the Nuits de Fourvière festival — two thousand years after the first one sat down in it. Standing in the stone seating, with the basilica's towers visible just above the rim, is as close as this walk gets to two different reasons people have kept climbing this hill for two millennia.

None of this requires December 8 specifically — the climb, the traboules and the ruins are there year-round. But doing this walk before dusk during the Fête des Lumières, December 5 to 8, 2026, means you already know what the candles are for by the time the first ones get lit.
