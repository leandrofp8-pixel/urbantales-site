The tower Anne Frank heard from hiding hides Rembrandt's grave

✎
Written by the Urban Tales team
October 5, 2026 · 6 min read

Anne Frank mentioned one specific clock more than twenty times in her diary — a bell tower she could hear from the Achterhuis but never once see. She had no way of knowing that the same church, a few hundred feet from where she was hiding, had already lost track of where it buried Rembrandt.

The tower is the Westertoren, the spire of the Westerkerk, and it's tall enough — about 85 meters, still the tallest church tower in Amsterdam — that anyone standing in the ticket line for the Anne Frank House can see it over the gable roofs without turning their head. Almost nobody in that line walks the extra block to go inside. The ones who do find a plain stone floor, a modest 20th-century plaque, and nothing to mark exactly where, underfoot, one of the most famous painters who ever lived is actually lying.

Walk there from Centraal Station and you pass block after block of the narrow, top-heavy canal houses this part of Amsterdam is known for. Most were built tall, deep, and tilted slightly forward on purpose, so that furniture hoisted up by rope to a top-floor window wouldn't scrape the façade on the way up — the staircases inside were always too steep and narrow for anything bigger than a chair. The iron hooks still bolted above the gables aren't decoration. People still use them, nearly four hundred years later, to get a sofa in through a third-floor window instead of up the stairs.

The bell Anne Frank worried about

During the occupation, German forces melted down church bells across the Netherlands for scrap metal — thousands of them pulled from towers that had rung for centuries. Anne wrote more than once about fearing the Westertoren's carillon would be next, the one piece of the outside world still reaching her through a blacked-out window. It wasn't taken. The bells that got her through two winters in hiding are the same ones still ringing over the Jordaan every fifteen minutes today.

A grave with an expiration date

Rembrandt spent his last years broke, having already buried his son Titus in a rented grave at the Westerkerk the year before. A rented grave in 17th-century Amsterdam was exactly what it sounds like: a cheaper plot leased for a fixed number of years, with the understanding that if nobody paid to renew it, the remains eventually got cleared to make room for someone else's. Rembrandt was buried the same way in October 1669. Neither lease got renewed. By the time Amsterdam got around to caring that one of its poorest former residents had become one of its most famous, the plot had long since been reused — church records narrow it down to a general area of the floor, not a spot you could actually put a boot on.

A rented grave bought you twenty years of being remembered. Rembrandt's ran out before Amsterdam decided he was worth remembering.

We asked a volunteer near the entrance exactly where, expecting a specific flagstone. She just swept her arm in a loose half-circle over the floor. "Somewhere under there" was the honest answer. It's an odd feeling, standing in a church that's completely certain about the man and completely vague about the body.

Try it free

The Anne Frank House itself sells out its timed-entry tickets online, often weeks out, and the walk-up queue on Prinsengracht can run close to an hour even for people who show up early. We lasted about twenty minutes in it before giving up and circling back later in the day, which is how we ended up with the extra hour to find the Westerkerk in the first place. The church, right next door, costs nothing to walk into and rarely holds more than a handful of visitors at once. It might be the only spot in central Amsterdam where two completely unrelated, centuries-apart pieces of history sit twenty feet from each other — and only one of them has a line.

The reason the house and the tower ended up neighbors at all is almost incidental: the Westerkerk had already been standing for three centuries by the time Otto Frank's company moved into the warehouse at Prinsengracht 263. Anne wasn't choosing a view when her family went into hiding there in 1942 — the annex just happened to sit close enough that the bells came through the walls anyway.

The tower is still visible from blocks away, over that same stretch of narrow canal houses. But the detail that sticks isn't the view, or the hooks, or even the bells. It's that a nearly 400-year-old church can be completely certain a wartime carillon survived, down to the chime, and still have no real idea exactly where it put one of the most famous painters who ever lived.
