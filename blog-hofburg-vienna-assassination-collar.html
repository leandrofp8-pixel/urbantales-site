The Hofburg story Vienna guides rush past

✎
Written by the Urban Tales team
September 16, 2026 · 6 min read

On February 18, 1853, a tailor's stiff collar saved an emperor's life on a street just outside the Hofburg's walls — and Vienna spent the next 26 years building a church to say thank you for it.

Most Hofburg tours spend their time on the Sisi Museum's mirrors and the Imperial Silver Collection, and skip the story of the afternoon a stranger walked up behind a 22-year-old Emperor Franz Joseph and tried to end the Habsburg dynasty with a kitchen knife. It happened a few hundred meters from the palace gates, and the only reason there's a Hofburg story to tell at all past 1853 is a piece of military uniform nobody designed to stop a blade.

A knife, a collar, and eleven seconds

Franz Joseph had taken the throne at eighteen after the revolutions of 1848, and by 1853 he was still touring the city's bastion walkways most afternoons with an aide at his side — no barricade of guards, no rope line, just a young monarch walking near the ramparts like it was any other Tuesday. A 22-year-old Hungarian journeyman tailor named János Libényi approached him from behind and drove a knife into the back of his neck.

What Libényi didn't account for was the emperor's collar. Austrian military dress uniforms of the period used a high, stiffened stand-collar — reinforced enough that the blade skidded instead of driving through. Franz Joseph went down bleeding but conscious. His aide-de-camp and a nearby butcher's assistant, Joseph Ettenreich, wrestled Libényi to the ground before he could strike again. The wound needed weeks to heal. The empire did not lose its emperor over a rooftop-lined Vienna street corner.

"A collar built for a parade ground ended up doing the job of a bulletproof vest."

The church built out of relief

Vienna's reaction wasn't just relief — it was a fundraising campaign. Franz Joseph's brother, Archduke Maximilian, proposed a votive church funded by public donations from across the empire as thanks for the emperor's survival. The result is the Votivkirche, a twin-spired neo-Gothic building that still stands on the Ringstrasse a short walk from the Hofburg, consecrated in 1879 — 26 years after the attack it commemorates, and finished just in time for Franz Joseph's silver wedding anniversary.

Ettenreich, the butcher's assistant who helped bring Libényi down, was ennobled for it and given the honorific "von Ettenreich." Libényi was tried and hanged within weeks. The whole episode reshaped how the Habsburgs moved through their own capital — visible walks along the bastions gave way to tighter escorts, at exactly the moment Vienna was about to tear those old fortification walls down anyway to build the Ringstrasse we walk today.

What's easy to miss now

Stand at the Hofburg today and the whole complex reads as calm: wide courtyards, a palace that's mostly museums and offices now, tourists photographing the Michaelertor gate. Nothing about it suggests a family that buried a murdered empress (Franz Joseph's wife, Elisabeth, was stabbed to death by an anarchist in Geneva in 1898) and a son who died by his own hand at a hunting lodge outside town in 1889. The 1853 attack is the one guides mention fastest, if they mention it at all, because it has the tidiest ending — the emperor lived, a church got built, a collar became a minor legend. The Habsburgs didn't get that lucky twice.

The Votivkirche is still there for anyone who wants to see where the gratitude went. The Hofburg is still there for anyone who wants to stand where the knife came out. Most people visiting do both without ever being told the two buildings are the same story.
