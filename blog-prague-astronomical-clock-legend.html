The blinding legend behind Prague's Astronomical Clock

✎
Written by the Urban Tales team
September 7, 2026 · 5 min read

Every hour, a mechanical skeleton yanks a bell rope above Prague's Old Town Square, and a couple hundred phones tilt up to film it. Almost none of those people know the story locals still tell about the man who built the Astronomical Clock the skeleton lives on — how the city allegedly blinded him the moment he finished it, and how he answered by breaking the thing so badly it sat dead for decades.

The Prague Astronomical Clock, the Orloj, has been mounted on the Old Town Hall tower since 1410, which makes it the oldest astronomical clock in the world still running. Everything else about it is at least a little contested — starting with who actually built it, and what the city supposedly did to him for it.

The clockmaker who saw too much

The version guides love goes like this: in 1490, a master clockmaker named Hanuš finished upgrading the mechanism with its full astronomical dial — sun, moon, zodiac, the works. Prague's councillors, terrified he'd build an even better one for a rival city, had him blinded before he ever left the tower. Hanuš asked to be led to the clock one last time. Once his hands found the gears, he reached in and jammed the mechanism so badly it reportedly didn't run again for close to a century.

Historians dispute most of it. Records suggest the real Jan Růže — the clockmaker later nicknamed Hanuš — kept working and likely died of ordinary old age, not sabotage. The blinding story shows up in written form only centuries later, and versions of the same tale (jealous city, blinded genius, sabotaged machine) turn up attached to clockmakers in other parts of Europe too. Romans tell the barber story about the Trevi Fountain. Praguers tell this one, and they tell it at the clock, every hour, to anyone standing close enough to hear it over the crowd.

"The clock has survived plague, war, and four different tellings of how it was built. The one thing it hasn't survived intact is its own apostles."

What the dial is actually doing

Under the legend, there's a genuinely strange piece of engineering. The Orloj doesn't just tell the hour — it tracks the position of the sun and moon against the zodiac, and it still runs on Old Czech Time, a 24-hour system that started counting at sunset rather than midnight. Four carved figures flank the dial: Vanity checks a mirror, a skeleton representing Death tips an hourglass and rings the bell, and two more figures act out vanity and greed getting quietly overruled by mortality, on the hour, every hour, since before Columbus sailed.

We showed up at 11:58 for the hourly show, certain we'd landed a great spot, and spent the first ten seconds of it squinting at the wrong window — the apostles only appear in the two dark openings above the dial, not on the dial itself, and by the time we found them half the show was already over. Now we know: get there five minutes early and pick your window before the crowd closes in around you.

The apostles who didn't survive the war

Here's the part almost nobody mentions, and it isn't legend — it's May 8, 1945. During the final days of the Prague Uprising, retreating German forces shelled the Old Town Hall and the fire that followed tore through the tower. The wooden Twelve Apostles that parade past two small windows above the dial each hour burned along with the hand-painted 1865 calendar ring underneath the clock. Nothing you see move on the clock face today is older than 1948 — sculptor Vojtěch Sucharda carved a new set of apostles from scratch, and the calendar ring is a later copy of the original.

So the clock in front of you now is a strange kind of original: a 616-year-old mechanism wearing a 78-year-old face, running on a system of hours nobody else uses, tied to a legend most historians think got attached to the wrong man. Locals still call it cursed anyway. Whether or not Hanuš existed the way the story says, something did keep this clock breaking down and getting stubbornly rebuilt for six centuries straight — and that's arguably the more impressive story.
