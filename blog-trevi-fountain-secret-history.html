The secret the Trevi Fountain guides don't tell you

✎
Written by the Urban Tales team
September 2, 2026 · 6 min read

Three million people throw a coin into the Trevi Fountain every year. Almost none of them know the fountain exists because a barber humiliated a pope — twice.

The water flowing through the Trevi today has been running through the same channel for over 2,000 years. It's called the Aqua Virgo, and the story of how it got its name — and how it nearly got a very different fountain built on top of it — is the kind of thing a tour guide will skip because there's a bus waiting and forty more people behind you.

A general, a thirsty army, and a girl who wasn't lost

In 19 BC, Roman general Marcus Agrippa was hunting for a new water source to feed his bathhouses. Legend says his soldiers, dying of thirst, were led to a spring by a young girl. Agrippa built an aqueduct on the spot and named it Aqua Virgo — the Virgin's Waters — in her honor. That aqueduct still feeds the fountain you see today, which is part of why the water looks so clean compared to some of Rome's other fountains.

"The fountain isn't decoration. It's the end of a 2,000-year-old plumbing project that Rome refused to let fail."

For centuries, the aqueduct simply emptied into a plain basin. It took a Baroque-era grudge match between two of Rome's most stubborn men to turn it into the spectacle it is now.

The barber's revenge

When Pope Clement XII announced a competition to redesign the fountain in 1730, the presumed winner was the celebrated architect Alessandro Galilei. Rome's guilds were furious — a Florentine had been chosen over its own sons. The uproar forced Clement to hand the job to a local, Nicola Salvi, who had never built anything of this scale in his life.

Salvi spent the next 30 years on the fountain, dying before it was finished. Local legend claims a stubborn barber near the site so annoyed Salvi with his opinions on the design that Salvi built the fountain's tall central stone — the Ace of Cups — specifically to block the barber's view of it forever. Historians dispute it. Romans tell it anyway, every single day, to anyone who'll listen. If you stand at the fountain's left edge, you can still see the stone in question — it's worth deciding for yourself if it really looks like it was built out of spite.

Why the coin still works

The tradition of throwing a coin over your left shoulder to guarantee a return trip to Rome wasn't part of the original design — it grew out of older Roman customs of offering coins to water spirits for safe travel. Today the fountain collects roughly €3,000 a day, all of it donated directly to Caritas, a Catholic charity that runs a supermarket for Rome's poor.

So the next time you're standing at the edge with a coin in hand, you're not just making a wish. You're the latest link in a chain that runs from a thirsty Roman legion, through a feuding architect and a spiteful barber, to a charity supermarket two thousand years later.
