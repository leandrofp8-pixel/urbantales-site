A self-guided walk through ancient Rome, from the Colosseum to Capitoline Hill

✎
Written by the Urban Tales team
October 28, 2026 · 6 min read

The Colosseum could hold as many as 80,000 people, and the floor they stood above hid more than 60 wooden lifts that could make a bear appear to rise out of solid ground on cue. This self-guided Colosseum and Roman Forum walk starts there and ends six stops later, at a balcony no plaque bothers to point out — about 2.3 km, roughly 30 minutes of actual walking if you don't go inside anything along the way.

Start on the Colosseum's south side, away from the metro exit crowd, where you can see all four surviving tiers of arches at once without a tour group in the frame. In summer the ticket line forms well before opening; a combined Colosseum–Forum–Palatine ticket, valid for 24 hours, means you only queue once instead of three times.

The trapdoors nobody warns you about

Gladiators and animals didn't enter through the gates everyone photographs — they came up from underneath, on cue. The hypogeum, a two-level warren of tunnels and cages beneath the arena floor, held a system of ropes, pulleys and counterweights that could lift a caged leopard to the sand in seconds, timed to a trumpet. A retractable awning called the velarium, rigged by off-duty Roman navy sailors, could shade the whole bowl from the sun in under a minute. None of the original wooden floor survives; what you see from the upper tiers today is the hypogeum itself, laid bare.

Leave past the Arch of Titus side, and the Arch of Constantine is already visible below — the triple-arched one standing free in the middle of the road rather than attached to anything.

A monument built from other men's monuments

Constantine's builders didn't have the decades a triumphal arch like this usually took, so most of its reliefs are spolia — stripped from monuments built two centuries earlier for Trajan, Hadrian and Marcus Aurelius. The theft is easy to spot once you know to look: the older medallions and panels have the deep, precise carving of imperial Rome at its wealthiest, while the narrow strips added for Constantine are flatter and cruder, cut by workshops that had lost generations of that skill. It's a 1,700-year-old patchwork pretending to be one piece.

The flowers on Caesar's grave

Follow Via Sacra, the Forum's old central street, toward the cluster of columns on the right. Most of what's left here is foundations and broken marble, but one low, curved stone wall usually has a small crowd around it even when the rest of the Forum is empty — and fresh flowers tucked into its cracks. It marks the Temple of Caesar, built over the spot where a grieving crowd cremated Julius Caesar's body in 44 BC on a pyre they built themselves, before the Senate could stop them. There's no sign explaining the flowers. People just keep leaving them.

The view Augustus chose

Climb toward the Palatine Hill, where Rome's first emperors built their homes, and the ruins open out over the long oval scar of the Circus Maximus below — you're standing close to where the starting gates once stood, which is also roughly why Augustus chose to live here. Excavators found his private study in 1969, its walls still painted with small, faded garden scenes meant to make a windowless room feel like it opened onto one.

A square that isn't actually square

Head down the hill's far side toward the Capitoline, up the shallow ramp Michelangelo designed to make the climb feel like an entrance. The piazza at the top is trapezoidal, not square — wider at the far end than near the stairs — but the star-patterned pavement he laid across it, and the matching facades on either side, trick your eye into reading it as a perfect, symmetrical oval. At its center, a replica she-wolf nurses two infants in bronze; the original inside the museum behind it was long dated to the 5th century BC, until a 2006 study of its casting method suggested it might be medieval instead. Nobody has settled the argument.

The balcony is still there

From the piazza's far corner, the white marble mass of the Vittoriano monument fills the view below — locals call it "the wedding cake" or "the typewriter," never warmly, since building it after 1885 meant tearing down a slice of the medieval neighborhood that stood there first. Just past it, on Piazza Venezia, sits Palazzo Venezia, a 15th-century building whose balcony still juts out exactly as it did when Mussolini used it to address the crowds gathered below through the 1920s, '30s and early '40s. Nothing marks which window it is; you just have to already know.

Via del Corso runs north from here toward the Pantheon and, a few streets further, the Trevi Fountain — close enough to fold into the same afternoon.
